# REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

## 2.1 Staff Training and Development

Organizations across sectors have long recognized that continuously developing their workforce is essential for effectiveness. Noe et al. (2023) state that training and development efforts aim to ensure that employees have the skills they need to perform their tasks. This is especially important in mission-driven organizations, where staff competence directly affects the quality of services provided to communities. Boxall and Purcell (2022) argue that Strategic Human Resource Management is a purposeful and systematic process for applying HR practices to achieve the best performance from people in support of organizational goals. In this context, training is not just a compliance task; it is a long-term investment in staff. Additionally, Fernandez and Gallardo-Gallardo (2021) highlight that the ability to analyze and use workforce data enables HR leaders to make informed decisions based on training records. Together, these insights support the need for the Social Development Unit (SDU) to have not only a record of staff training activities but also a system that connects training data to planning. This is exactly what the proposed SDU-STMS aims to fulfill.

### 2.1.1 Capacity building in Organizations

Staff training is one of the ways capacity building is delivered in organizations. The United Nation (UN) defines capacity-building as a comprehensive effort to strengthen the abilities and instincts of organizations, ensuring they can adapt to rapid change, and the national council of Nonprofit (2023) defines capacity building as the investment in developing the competencies and skills of nonprofit organizations and their leaders, enabling them to become more effective and sustainable. Musau (2024), in a study on digital transformation in non-profit organizations published in the Advanced International Journal of Multidisciplinary Research, found that organizations which ensured employees received systematic training in information and communication technology (ICT) use were better able to utilize digital tools effectively in their activities. The UN-Habitat Capacity Development Training Unit (2020) further affirms that digital tools have changed the way training and capacity building are offered in organizations, and that remote learning offered by technology can lower the expenses associated with on-site training while also allowing capacity building initiatives to reach a larger audience. This finding is particularly relevant to multi-center organizations like the Social Development Unit (SDU) of Ateneo De Zamboanga University, where staff are distributed across six (6) specialized centers with diverse program requirements.

### 2.1.2 Training Tracking in the SDU Context

The need to record and track staff trainings is directly mirrored in the local context of the institution of Ateneo de Zamboanga University, where the Social Development Unit (SDU) faces unique hiring dynamics or the way offices build its team with different degrees, experiences or backgrounds. Because the unit often employs personnel from diverse academic backgrounds who are not licensed Social Workers to execute specialized social work functions, training serves as an important bridge to ensure staff gain essential competencies in areas such as Community Organizing and Project Management. The SDU works across six (6) specialized centers, and each center needs to record and track the trainings its staff attend and the trainings its staff conduct. Bringing these records together in one system is necessary for the unit to see who has been trained, in what subject, and where gaps remain.

Bryan et al. (2020) and Samad and Ahmad (2022) state that organizational capacities encompassing staff management, operational systems, and leadership are directly linked to organization's ability to achieve its mission and maintain itself over time. This is especially important for mission-driven institutions like the Social Development Unit (SDU), where the quality of staff competencies directly determines the effectiveness of community involvement delivered to marginalized sectors, displaced communities, and Indigenous People across Zamboanga City.

### 2.1.3 Challenges of Manual Training Record Keeping

Despite the growing need for systematic training record keeping, many organizations, notably those in the nonprofit and social development sectors, continue to handle their staff training records manually using spreadsheets. These methods present a variety of operational challenges that affect both data quality and organizational responsiveness.

One of the most critical challenges with manual tracking systems is their considerable susceptibility to human error. Expiration Reminder (2025), in a technical analysis of training tracking failures, documents how spreadsheet-based compliance management is consistently prone to copy-paste errors, formula breaks, version inconsistencies, and missing entries, and errors that are frequently silent and only discovered after problems have occurred. These types of errors are especially damaging to multi-staff, multi-office environments, where data is entered by a variety of users with various levels of data literacy and accuracy.

The administrative burden of manual data management is also high. According to a 2023 study by Deloitte cited in HROne (2025), HR professionals spend an average of fourteen (14) hours per week on manual data entry and error correction alone, which equates to nearly two (2) full workdays dedicated to tasks with no strategic value. For social development organizations that already have limited administrative capacity, such inefficiencies restrict the time and energy available for critical duties. Version control is another challenging issue in shared spreadsheet setups. In a review of HR data management issues, TimeOffCloud (2024) notes that collaborative spreadsheet systems sometimes produce contradictory data versions when multiple users access and edit a document concurrently or asynchronously. This lack of a single source of truth is especially problematic for the SDU, because the Unit Director requires consolidated, accurate views of staff training records from six (6) distinct centers to make informed decisions.

Aside from data accuracy challenges, manual systems lack the infrastructure necessary for proactive monitoring and automated reporting. D2L (2025) and Expiration Reminder (2025) both state that organizations that rely on spreadsheets for training oversight are unable to generate automated alerts for approaching deadlines, apply role-based access controls to ensure data security, or produce dynamic dashboards without significant manual involvement. This reactive approach, in which faults are detected only by manual review, is incompatible with the level of organizational accountability and responsiveness required by mission-driven organizations such as the Social Development Unit (SDU).

Nonprofit organizations face a particularly acute version of these challenges. According to the HR Consulting Group (2024), organizations with wide program portfolios and small administrative teams commonly discover that manual HR processes become complicated, which divert leadership attention away from important social mission activities. The combination of these challenges such as the data inaccuracy, administrative complexity, version conflicts, and the lack of automated monitoring offers a convincing case for moving away from manual spreadsheet-based tracking and toward integrated digital training management systems.

### 2.1.4 Training Data for Management Decisions

The value of personnel data goes beyond record-keeping; it is a crucial input for organizational decision-making. According to HR management literature, the ability of leadership to make accurate, timely, and evidence-based decisions is directly related to the quality and accessibility of workforce information (Fernandez and Gallardo-Gallardo, 2021; Ulrich and Dulebohn, 2015).

In a review of HR analytics literature, Zebua et al. (2024) emphasize that the introduction of Human Resource analytics has transformed the way organizations manage their workforce, allowing for data-driven decision-making and planning where intuition and manual review were previously available. Their synthesis confirms that organizations that incorporate analytics into HR processes achieve higher decision-making accuracy, lower biases, and better predictive capabilities for workforce trends. Outcomes that are important for organizations like the SDU that must continuously align staff competencies with evolving program requirements. More specifically, in the field of training management, data-driven analytics is critical in identifying skill gaps and prioritizing development interventions. According to a study published in the IRE Journals (2024), organizations that include data analytics into HR management tasks have higher decision-making accuracy and predictive capacities for workforce training trends. This analytical capability enables unit directors and supervisors to transition from reactive, compliance-based oversight to proactive planning, detecting where staff development initiatives are most needed before operational gaps turn into severe failures.

Frontiers in Psychology (2024), through a systematic review of decision-making methods in human resource management, points out that HR work rarely happens under ideal conditions. Most of it unfolds amid uncertainty, competing priorities, and incomplete information. What makes a real difference, the review argues, is having structured data to lean on. When HR practitioners are supported by organized, filterable information rather than scattered records, their decisions around performance management, talent development, and workforce planning become noticeably more grounded and consistent. In short, good data does not just save time but also it changes the quality of the choices being made.

This idea carries even more weight in a management context. Zhang and Chen (2024), as cited in a review published by MDPI (2025), observe that the shift toward digital HR tools is not just a matter of efficiency, it fundamentally changes what HR leadership is able to do. When managers no longer have to spend hours manually pulling together records, they can actually focus on what the data is telling them and act on it. For the SDU, this is exactly the kind of shift the proposed SDU-STMS is designed to support. Rather than leaving the Unit Director to sort through spreadsheets at the end of an audit cycle, the system provides a centralized, up-to-date view of staff training that makes better decision-making a realistic part of day-to-day operations. In practice, this means using the training records to identify which trainings staff still need and to decide which staff to assign to future trainings.

In the nonprofit and social sector context specifically, Social Current (2025) highlights that organizations are increasingly adopting Performance and Quality Improvement (PQI) models that embed data-driven practices into how they evaluate and improve their work. By consistently tracking staff training participation and competency development, these organizations become better positioned to allocate resources wisely, strengthen their programs, and demonstrate accountability to funders and the communities they serve. For the SDU, an organization whose work directly impacts marginalized sectors, displaced communities, and Indigenous Peoples, this is not just a best practice. It is a responsibility.

### 2.1.5 Participation Roles as Evidence of Experience

Not all training participation is the same. The role a staff member plays in a training event, together with the number of times they have taken that role, provides evidence of experience with a competency. Roles like facilitator, organizer, resource speaker, and participant are seen as different ways to engage. Each role requires a different level of skill and knowledge.

Acorn PLMS (2026) sets training facilitators apart from regular participants by calling them process experts. These professionals do not just receive information; they actively shape the learning experience by guiding discussions, promoting knowledge sharing, and helping others learn from their experiences. This type of engagement requires a greater level of skill than just showing up. The facilitator must master the subject matter, manage group dynamics, and make sure everyone in the room meets the learning objectives. Edstellar (n.d.) also highlights that the facilitator's role goes beyond delivering content. It includes creating a lively learning environment that boosts active participation, encourages critical thinking, and promotes the practical use of skills. These are all key aspects of strong professional growth.

The organizer role plays a unique part in experience building. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) training preparation guide states that the logistics and design tasks of training organizers, which include setting the agenda and securing resources and anticipating participant needs, create a kind of organizational and procedural skill that is seldom gained through passive attendance. A staff member who takes charge of how a training event runs from the ground up must fully understand its purpose and content. This means turning subject knowledge into structured learning experiences for others.

For staff in the participant role, how they engage matters a lot. The FAO guide states that hands-on, participatory activities, where participants draw their own conclusions through discussion, questioning, and collaboration, lead to more meaningful and lasting learning outcomes than passive lectures. A mixed-methods study published in Nursing Reports (2024) reinforces this point. It found a clear difference in learning progression, depth of reflection, and knowledge application between experienced facilitators and those who were just passive participants in the same training events. This shows that taking an active role in training produces richer experience than merely observing.

The broader literature on professional learning supports this layered view of participation. The Journal of Professional Capital and Community (2026) notes that facilitators in professional learning settings must handle organizational tasks, such as connecting, encouraging, and coordinating group activities. They also need content expertise and the ability to inspire deep, reflective inquiry among participants. These are not occasional benefits; they are important skills developed specifically due to the demands placed on facilitators.

This range of participation roles is important for the SDU-STMS. By recording not only whether a staff member attended a training event but also their specific role, and the number of sessions they have taken in that role, the system builds evidence of experience for each competency. A staff member who has led a session on Community Organizing has more experience with that competency compared to someone who just attended the same session. The role and the number of sessions are evidence of experience for a competency, not a demonstrated skill by themselves. They show where a person's experience lies and help the Unit Director and Office Head make more informed and thoughtful decisions about each staff member's growth path.

### 2.1.6 Skills and Competency Matching Systems

Beyond recording that a staff member attended a training, organizations increasingly require systems that can match staff competencies to organizational needs. Bibi et al. (2021), in a study published in PLOS ONE, propose a skills management framework built around four sequential stages: identifying an organization's required competencies, assessing employees' acquired skills, matching required and acquired skills to available tasks, and recommending targeted development where gaps exist. The study notes that most organizations fail to practice systematic skills management for two reasons, employee skills are not effectively evaluated and documented, and existing tools are insufficient to manage the matching process, a finding that mirrors the SDU's current reliance on informal memory and resume review to identify who is qualified to serve as a resource speaker or facilitator.

Competencies and knowledge areas are recorded separately in the system. A competency is a capability that a staff member can perform, while a knowledge area is a subject that the staff member is familiar with. The two are related but they are not the same, and the system keeps them in separate catalogs so that a training can be tagged with the competencies it builds and the knowledge areas it covers.

Matching in the SDU-STMS means selecting staff for training assignments. Rather than requiring the Unit Director to recall which staff member has prior experience in a given subject area, as is currently the practice at the SDU, the system tags trainings with the competencies and knowledge areas they build and allows the Unit Director or Office Head to query which staff members hold a specific competency or knowledge area, and then select them for a training assignment. This directly addresses the documented pattern at the SDU wherein the same one or two centers are repeatedly assigned to external speaking requests because no searchable record of other centers' staff competencies exists.

### 2.1.7 Partner and Stakeholder Institution Tracking

Organizations that rely on external partners to sustain their programs face a distinct tracking challenge: partnerships are frequently more complex and networked than a single formal agreement implies. Taylor et al. (2023), in a multi-case qualitative study of business-nonprofit partnerships published in Frontiers in Health Services, found that partnership literature often assumes a simple, one-to-one relationship between an organization and its partner, when in practice a nonprofit's partnerships are embedded within a broader network of overlapping obligations and contributions. This finding is relevant to the SDU, whose six centers each maintain independent relationships with government agencies, non-government organizations, and private foundations, none of which are currently logged in a structured or centralized way.

The absence of structured partner tracking creates a downstream visibility problem for planning. Without a centralized record of which institutions are actively engaged and when existing agreements expire, an organization's leadership cannot easily assess its own external support network. The SDU-STMS Partner Institution module addresses this gap by maintaining a structured record for each partner organization, including the agreement documentation, an agreement expiry date with an alert when the expiry is near or past, and the lead implementing office that carries out the partnership.

### 2.1.8 Evaluating Conducted Trainings through Participant Feedback

The Kirkpatrick Model, one of the most widely applied training evaluation frameworks, distinguishes four levels of evaluation: Reaction, Learning, Behavior, and Results (Nawaz et al., 2022). Reaction measures how participants respond to a training, covering their satisfaction and their feedback on the event. Faisal-E-Alam and Islam (2025), in a study validating the hierarchical relationships between these levels, found that positive trainee reactions influence learning outcomes, which in turn produce behavioral change in the workplace, and that this individual-level behavioral change is what ultimately drives improved organizational performance, not attendance or satisfaction alone.

This distinction is directly relevant to the training that the SDU's staff conduct. When a staff member conducts a training, the participants who took part provide feedback, and the unit records that feedback as an evaluation of the training event. This follows the Reaction level of the Kirkpatrick Model, because the evaluation captures how the participants responded to the training that was conducted. The evaluation is a record of a training event and its participant feedback; it is not a rating of an individual staff member. Attending a training is not rated, and no numeric rating is produced for a person. The evaluation module is scoped to the trainings the SDU's staff conduct, so that the unit can see how those trainings were received and use the feedback to improve future trainings.

## 2.2 MIS, Architecture, and Data

This section examines the technical concepts that form the structural foundation of the proposed SDU-STMS. It reviews the nature and role of Management Information Systems in organizational settings, the architectural model most appropriate for a web-based institutional system, the importance of role-based access control in managing multi-user environments, the value of data visualization and dashboard analytics in supporting managerial reporting and the identification of training needs, and the role of notifications and automated alerts. Together, these concepts establish the technical rationale for the design choices made in the development of the SDU-STMS and explain why a centralized, database-driven web system is the most appropriate solution to the SDU's current operational challenges.

### 2.2.1 Management Information System (MIS)

A Management Information System (MIS) is defined as an integrated system designed to collect, process, store, and disseminate organizational data to support daily operations and managerial decision-making (Ahmed, 2026; Marar, n.d.). Its primary role is to serve as a central repository of information, centralizing vast amounts of raw data into a structured format that helps managers make accurate and well-informed decisions (Cohesity, 2026). By relying on well-managed databases, an MIS establishes a single source of truth, minimizes data redundancy, and transforms scattered business data into meaningful insights that support strategic organizational planning (Chitimbe, n.d.).

The implementation of an MIS directly connects to the Social Development Unit's (SDU) current challenge, the necessary shift from manual tracking via Google Sheets to an automated database. The SDU-STMS functions as an MIS by consolidating staff training records from six centers into a single accessible platform, enabling the Unit Director to monitor compliance and make informed decisions.

In its application to this study, an MIS directly resolves the critical bottlenecks present in the SDU's current manual Personnel Audit Forms. Presently, the organization suffers from "Inefficient data input" because staff members are required to manually encode comprehensive details such as names, positions, assigned programs, and attained competencies at the start of each academic year, consuming time that would be better spent on primary responsibilities. Moreover, this manual method results in a "High Risk of Data Inconsistency and Human Error," making the data highly vulnerable to simple misspellings, duplicate entries, and collaborative version control conflicts. Poor information quality derived from such errors can undermine decision accuracy and managerial confidence (Ahmed, 2026). An MIS solves these exact issues by automating data workflows, establishing standard procedures for data entry, and using database validations to prevent human errors and eliminate repetitive manual processing Cohesity, 2026).

MIS provides the centralized data environment the Unit Director needs, moving away from scattered, isolated spreadsheets. Rather than relying on a static and decentralized tracking method, this centralized architecture ensures high data integrity and provides real-time, filtered access to staff competencies, empowering the Unit Director to proactively manage workforce development without having to manually sift through rows of data.

### 2.2.2 System Architecture and Framework

A system architecture defines the structural design of a software system, describing how its components are organized, how they interact, and how data flows between them. Setyawati and Hariri (2021) describe a web-based management information system as a database-intensive and transaction-intensive information system that is implemented and made accessible over the web through user access via web browsers or mobile devices. In this model, data is obtainable through web interfaces organized into navigational hierarchies, with services available for retrieving and modifying data from the underlying database. This foundational definition is directly applicable to the SDU-STMS, which is designed as a web-accessible platform where the Unit Director, Office Head, and Staff interact through their respective role-based interfaces while all data flows into and from a single centralized database.

The architectural model most appropriate for such a system is the client-server architecture. Nyabuto (2024) defines this as a software model through which resources and requests are serviced over a network, where the client requests a resource, and the server receives the request, processes it, and responds appropriately. A key advantage of this model is that all data is saved in a central location and accessed from multiple locations simultaneously, which allows for increased scalability and centralized resource management. Within the client-server model, the three-tier architecture is the most relevant variant for the SDU-STMS. Nyabuto (2024) identifies the three components of this architecture as the Client Tier, which handles the display of information and sends data to other tiers for processing; the Logic or Application Tier, which processes requests received from the client and retrieves data from the data tier; and the Data Tier, which stores and retrieves information from the database using a database management system.

This separation of concerns produces improved scalability, better security, and increased performance, since the presentation, application, and data layers each operate independently. In a comparable system, Sankoh et al. (2026) implemented a three-tier architecture for a web-based industrial placement management system, consisting of a presentation layer built with HTML5, CSS3, and JavaScript; a logic layer handling server-side processing; and a data layer using MySQL for persistent storage. This modular architecture supported scalability and maintainability, and the system achieved an average response time of less than two seconds and data accuracy exceeding 98%, confirming the viability of this architectural approach for institutional management systems. Where Sankoh et al. (2026) validate the three-tier pattern itself, Sri Kanna et al. (2026) provide evidence specific to the data layer the SDU-STMS adopts: their Smart HR Management System applied the same three-tier structure with a PostgreSQL-backed data layer and role-based access control enforced at both the application and database levels, sustaining API response times under 120 milliseconds against a simulated load of 10,000 concurrent records. Together, these two comparable systems support both the architectural pattern and the specific relational database engine the SDU-STMS relies on.

The superiority of a structured relational database over a flat spreadsheet system is further affirmed by Shen (2020), who establishes a fundamental distinction between files and databases: file systems are suited for storing mostly unrelated data, whereas a database is used for storing structured and related data. Unlike a spreadsheet, a database enforces a defined schema where each column has a specified data type and each row must conform to it, preventing the inconsistencies that arise when multiple users input data freely. Noble (2025) further notes that databases enforce rules such as unique keys, required fields, and referential integrity, while updates follow ACID properties, the Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, and Durability, that make sure that data remains reliable even under concurrent access. In contrast, spreadsheets are prone to duplicate entries, inconsistent values, and broken formulas, particularly in shared, multi-user environments. For the SDU, whose six centers currently enter data into a shared Google Sheet with no schema enforcement, these structural limitations are precisely the source of the data inconsistency and version control problems identified in Section 1.2.

In application to this study, the SDU-STMS adopts a three-tier client-server web architecture supported by a centralized relational database. This integrated database architecture ensures that all modules, training inputs, competency gap mapping, notification alerts, and automated reporting scripts are interconnected within a single data environment. Any data encoded by a staff member is immediately available to the Unit Director's monitoring dashboard, eliminating the need for manual data transfers and producing the accurate, real-time visibility that the SDU's current system cannot provide.

### 2.2.3 Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)

Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) is a non-discretionary access control mechanism that bases access decisions on the functions a user is authorized to perform within an organization. Unlike traditional models that rely on individual identity, modern RBAC implementations assign permissions to specific roles, ensuring that users can only execute transactions necessary for their professional responsibilities. This approach is increasingly critical for web-based systems that handle institutional data, as it enforces the principle of least privilege-minimizing the risk of unauthorized data exposure or accidental modification. For the SDU-STMS, this model is essential to move away from the "flat" permission structure of shared spreadsheets, where all users currently have equal and unrestricted access to sensitive personnel data.

The practical effectiveness of RBAC in institutional web-based systems is affirmed by several recent studies. Cardos et al. (2025), in their evaluation of RBAC implementation within the SEAIT OJT Evaluation and Feedback System at South East Asian Institute of Technology, found that role-specific access restrictions meaningfully reduced the risk of unauthorized data access and enhanced the overall privacy of sensitive academic records. Their survey of 110 respondents yielded a mean satisfaction score of 3.01 out of 4.00, confirming that users recognized the effectiveness of role-based permissions in maintaining data confidentiality. The study further identified that aligning system access with institutional roles improved both security compliance and user trust, outcomes that are directly applicable to the SDU's need to restrict staff data visibility across its six specialized centers. In a comparable institutional context, Chaudhari et al. (2026) implemented RBAC in a Patient Record Management System wherein user roles like Administrator, Doctor, and Patient were each assigned distinct access privileges: administrators managing system users and configurations, doctors accessing only their assigned patients' records, and patients restricted to viewing their own information. This tiered structure prevented unauthorized access, reduced administrative workload, and improved data security across the system, demonstrating that hierarchical role separation is a proven and practical approach to controlling sensitive information in multi-user institutional environments.

In application to this study, the SDU-STMS implements RBAC across its three roles, the Unit Director, the Office Head, and Staff, to ensure that each user can only access data relevant to their assigned role. The Unit Director holds the broadest access, encompassing system-wide monitoring, account management, training assignment, and report generation. Office Head are restricted to the training records and training data of staff within their respective centers, preventing cross-center data exposure. Staff members are limited to managing their own profiles, encoding their training records, and responding to notifications. Critically, new user accounts are placed under pending status upon registration and are only activated upon Unit Director approval, ensuring that no unverified user gains access to the system. This access control structure directly addresses the SDU's data privacy concern raised during the interview phase and ensures that the integrity of organizational data is protected throughout all system interactions.

### 2.2.4 Data Visualization and Dashboard Analytics

Data visualization refers to the process of transforming raw organizational data into graphical, interactive, and filterable formats such as charts, heatmaps, and dynamic dashboards that allow managers to identify patterns and act on information quickly. Dashboards serve as tools for overseeing organizational performance, presenting data consolidated from various sources to offer a comprehensive view, eliminating the need for managers to go through multiple reports and compile data themselves. In the context of a Management Information System, this visual layer is what separates a passive data repository from an active decision-support platform. Rather than storing records that must be manually retrieved and sorted, a well-designed dashboard surfaces the right information to the right user at the right time, making it an essential component of any integrated management system.

Beyond their role as display tools, interactive dashboards directly improve the quality and speed of managerial decision-making. Traditional tabular reports, while precise, often lead to cognitive overload due to excessive reliance on numerical comprehension and manual data extraction. In contrast, interactive dashboards play a crucial role in enhancing managerial decision-making by providing real-time analytics, performance tracking, and predictive insights. This is further supported by Hjelle et al. (2024), whose experimental study involving 524 participants confirmed that the completeness of information presented through dashboard visualizations is critical for enhancing decision-making quality, as it reduces the perceived complexity of tasks and enhances information satisfaction among decision-makers. These findings are directly relevant to the SDU's context, where the Unit Director currently has no automated view of staff training and must manually process spreadsheet rows to generate any meaningful summary.

In direct application to this study, the dashboard of the SDU-STMS is the practical implementation of these visualization principles. The dashboard is designed to consolidate all training records from the SDU's six centers into a single, filterable interface, allowing the Unit Director to produce training reports and view record types, participation roles, and the trainings still needed, without any manual data processing. The filter module makes things even better by letting the Director search for information based on a specific office, a person's role, or a certain timeframe. This kind of reporting turns the old, slow process into a system where the unit can identify training needs and decide which trainings and staff to prioritize at any moment.

### 2.2.5 Notifications and Automated Alerts

Notifications and automated alerts are a further function of an integrated training management system. D2L (2025) and Expiration Reminder (2025) both state that organizations that rely on spreadsheets for training oversight are unable to generate automated alerts for approaching deadlines without significant manual involvement. Building on this, automated alerts reduce the need for manual follow-up because the system can notify the right person when a deadline is near or when an action is required.

In application to this study, the SDU-STMS provides automatic notices for account decisions, training assignments, certificate uploads, and partner agreement expiry, and it allows the Unit Director and the Office Head to send one-way announcements to the staff within their scope. This keeps the unit informed without manual tracking and supports the same proactive monitoring that the manual spreadsheet approach cannot provide.

At the national level, one of the most relevant existing systems is the Integra HRIS Government Edition developed by Cosmotech Philippines, Inc., a PH-built Human Resource Information System designed specifically for Philippine government agencies in compliance with Civil Service Commission standards. The system covers a broad range of HR functions including employee records management, payroll, time and attendance, and notably, training and competency management. The training module allows users to create an unlimited number of trainings based on competencies for further enhancement, provides a summary of the training required per employee based on the competency requirements per position, and has a facility for the Personnel Development Committee for easy approval, identifying, and monitoring of training requirements through the Employee Self-Service portal. While these features are similar to the SDU-STMS's notification module, the Integra HRIS Government Edition is a comprehensive enterprise HR system that covers payroll, leave management, performance evaluation, and biometric attendance far beyond the scope of what the SDU requires. It is also designed for large government agencies with full IT infrastructure and HR departments. It is offered free of charge only to eligible government agencies, and this does not extend to a small, multi-center nonprofit unit like the SDU, making it operationally and financially inaccessible to the unit. ### 2.3.3 Institutional System: ADZU HRADO Portal

Narrowing the funnel further from the national level, it is necessary to examine the existing human resource platform already available within Ateneo de Zamboanga University itself: the HRADO (Human Resources and Administrative Office) portal. Since the SDU operates as a unit within ADZU, any proposal for a new staff training system must first address why an existing institutional tool cannot already serve this purpose.

Interview data gathered from the APC Program Coordinator, an SDU staff member with four years of tenure in her current role, clarified that the HRADO portal in practice consists of two functionally separate components. The first is a leave-filing system, which is mandatory for all university personnel and is used strictly for salary and attendance-related purposes. The second is a training-entry feature, which technically exists within the portal but has never been required or enforced by HR for actual use in her own words, "hindi rin siya required sa amin by HR." Despite holding her position for four years, she reported never having used this training-entry feature at any point.

This gap produced a concrete operational consequence: when the APC Program Coordinator needed documented proof of her training history to support a promotion application, she was unable to retrieve it from HRADO and instead had to manually search through several years of physical training certificates to compile the required record. When asked whether a Director-approved, system-generated printable export of the kind proposed for the SDU-STMS would have been sufficient for that same HR promotion requirement, she confirmed that it would have been, and that it would have eliminated the manual search entirely. This finding is significant for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that the absence of a functioning training-record system is not a limitation of institutional will or policy, since a technical feature for this purpose already exists within HRADO, but a limitation of adoption and enforcement, since HR itself never required its use. Second, it confirms that the SDU's specific operational need, a reliable, exportable, system-generated, Director-approved printable export, is not currently met anywhere within the university's existing digital infrastructure, despite a superficially similar feature being technically present. The SDU-STMS does not compete with or duplicate HRADO's leave-filing function; it fills the specific, unaddressed gap in verifiable, exportable training documentation that HRADO's own underused feature was never positioned to solve for the SDU's six specialized centers.

## 2.4 Findings

The review of related literature and existing systems leads to the following findings.

1. Manual spreadsheets cannot support training record keeping across offices. The literature on manual tracking shows that spreadsheets are prone to human error, version conflicts, and a lack of automated monitoring, and these problems are magnified when records are kept by multiple users across the SDU's six specialized centers. Manual methods do not give the Unit Director a single, reliable view of staff training.

2. A central relational database with role-based access, dashboards, and notifications is justified. The literature on MIS and system architecture confirms that a centralized relational database provides a single source of truth with schema enforcement, referential integrity, and role-based access, while dashboards and automated alerts support reporting and the identification of training needs. This supports moving the SDU away from shared spreadsheets.

3. TalentLMS, Cornerstone, and Integra do not offer the combined role tagging and multi-office hierarchy. Although these platforms provide training management, competency tracking, and analytics, none of them combines participation role tagging or a Unit Director and Office Head hierarchy across offices; for competency and knowledge tagging, assignment of staff by competency, and evaluation of conducted trainings, no documentation of this feature was found in the materials reviewed.

4. HRADO exists but is not used for training records. The university's HRADO portal contains a training-entry feature, but it has never been required or adopted by HR, and even long-tenured SDU staff have not used it, resorting instead to manual searches through physical certificates. HRADO therefore does not currently meet the SDU's need for a reliable training record.

5. SDU-STMS is a targeted response to that gap. The proposed SDU-STMS is designed around the SDU's actual operational requirements: recording trainings, tagging participation roles, competencies, and knowledge areas, assigning staff by competency, evaluating conducted trainings through participant feedback, tracking partner agreements, and giving the Unit Director and Office Head role-based access across offices. It addresses a genuine and documented organizational gap rather than duplicating an existing system.
